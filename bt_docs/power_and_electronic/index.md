When you plug in your 4S LiPo battery, the voltage does not instantly jump to a stable level. Instead, the interaction between the battery connection, the Flight Control Unit (FCU) capacitor, and your low-voltage divider creates a distinct voltage curve over the first few milliseconds.Here is exactly what happens to the voltage over time when you connect the power, and how it impacts your companion computer.1. Use code with caution.Phase 1: The RC Delay & Inrush Current (0 to 1 ms)What happens: The instant the metal contacts touch, your FCU’s large capacitor acts like a temporary short circuit. A massive rush of current (inrush current) flows from the battery to fill the capacitor.The Voltage Curve: Because the capacitor is absorbing energy, the voltage at the FCU input rises at an exponential rate (an RC ramp). It takes a fraction of a millisecond to climb toward the battery's nominal voltage.Phase 2: The Contact Bounce & Inductive Spike (~1ms to 3ms)What happens: When you plug a connector in by hand, the metal contacts actually bounce microscopically a few times before mating firmly.The Voltage Curve: This rapid connecting and disconnecting, combined with the inductance of your battery wires, creates voltage spikes and ringing. The voltage can transiently shoot higher than 16.8V and then dip back down rapidly.Phase 3: Stabilization (~5ms and beyond)What happens: The capacitor becomes fully charged, the mechanical connection stabilizes, and the high-frequency ringing dampens out.The Voltage Curve: The voltage flattens out into a rock-solid 14.8V – 16.8V (depending on the 4S LiPo's state of charge).2. How the Pololu & Companion Computer RespondBecause you have implemented the Under-Voltage Lockout (UVLO) circuit (120kΩ / 10kΩ), your system handles this startup timeline remarkably well:Ignoring the Initial Low Voltage: During the absolute first micro-seconds of Phase 1, the voltage is below 13.0V. The Pololu EN pin stays below 1.0V, keeping the companion computer completely OFF. This protects the computer from trying to boot up on a sagging, weak voltage.The "Instant-On" Risk: The voltage will cross your 13.0V threshold incredibly fast (usually in under 1 or 2 milliseconds). Because the Pololu regulator responds to the EN pin within microseconds, it will turn on almost immediately during Phase 2, right when the voltage is still ringing or spiking.The Capacitor's Secret Benefit: Your FCU input capacitor doesn't just delay the voltage rise; it acts as a low-pass filter. It absorbs the high-frequency voltage spikes from Phase 2, smoothing out the power before it reaches your UVLO resistor network. This ensures the Pololu EN pin doesn't see a wildly fluctuating voltage.3. Is This Enough Delay for a Companion Computer?Mechanically, yes; Logically, maybe not.While the UVLO circuit ensures the companion computer never sees "brownout" voltages during the plug-in sequence, a 5-millisecond delay might still be too fast if you want the FCU to fully boot up first.If your goal is to ensure the flight controller is fully alive, stable, and communicating before the companion computer turns on, you need a True Boot Delay (seconds, not milliseconds).Would you like to know how to add a time-delay circuit (using a capacitor on the EN pin) or use an FCU GPIO pin to hold the companion computer off until the flight software is fully booted?
